Week 4: regression

≈ 10 min

Regression measures how an outcome changes with one or more predictors. It is the most widely used method in medical statistics, and the basis of genetic association studies.

From tests to models

A test tells you whether there is evidence of an effect. A model tells you how big the effect is, and lets you ask about one thing while allowing for others. This week you meet the regression models that do most of the work in medical research and in genetics: linear regression for continuous outcomes, and logistic regression for yes-or-no outcomes. At the end you will see that the tests of last week are special cases of these models.

You will use the FAMuSS data again, and the leukaemia data at the very end. Read both, as in the last two weeks. Correlation

≈ 25 min

The correlation coefficient measures how closely two continuous variables follow a straight line. It is a good start, but it can’t tell you how much one changes with the other.

Taller people tend to be heavier. A scatter plot shows the relationship:

R
ggplot(fms, aes(x = Pre.height, y = Pre.weight)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.4)

The correlation coefficient, r (Pearson’s correlation), measures how closely two variables follow a straight line. It goes from −1 (a perfect straight line going down) through 0 (no straight-line relationship) to +1 (a perfect straight line going up):

r = Σ (xi − x̄)(yi − ȳ) / √[ Σ (xi − x̄)² × Σ (yi − ȳ)² ]
positive when values above the mean of x tend to go with values above the mean of y

cor() calculates r (use = "complete.obs" leaves out the people missing either value), and cor.test() tests whether it differs from 0 and gives a confidence interval:

> cor(fms$Pre.height, fms$Pre.weight, use = "complete.obs")
[1] 0.5339761
> cor.test(fms$Pre.height, fms$Pre.weight)

	Pearson's product-moment correlation

data:  fms$Pre.height and fms$Pre.weight
t = 20.269, df = 1030, p-value < 2.2e-16
alternative hypothesis: true correlation is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.4888826 0.5762237
sample estimates:
      cor
0.5339761

r² (here 0.53² = 0.29) is the proportion of the variation in one variable that goes with variation in the other: 29% of the variation in weight goes with height.

Spearman’s correlation is the correlation of the ranks. It measures any relationship that goes consistently up or down, whether or not it is a straight line, and it isn’t thrown by outliers. It warns that it can’t compute an exact p-value with ties (people with the same values), so it uses an approximation, which is fine with this many people:

> cor.test(fms$pre.BMI, fms$NDRM.CH, method = "spearman")

	Spearman's rank correlation rho

data:  fms$pre.BMI and fms$NDRM.CH
S = 206112394, p-value = 2.909e-11
alternative hypothesis: true rho is not equal to 0
sample estimates:
      rho
-0.207464

Warning message:
In cor.test.default(fms$pre.BMI, fms$NDRM.CH, method = "spearman") :
  cannot compute exact p-value with ties
Correlation is not causation

People with a higher BMI gained less strength, in percentage terms (Spearman’s ρ = −0.21). That doesn’t mean that a higher BMI causes smaller gains. Something else could cause both: men have a higher BMI on average and gained less, in percentage terms, than women. A variable like this is a confounder, and you will meet it again in chapter 3. Correlation also only measures one kind of relationship: always plot the data.

Simple linear regression

≈ 40 min

A regression line tells you how much an outcome changes, on average, for each unit of a predictor, and how sure you can be about it.

The model

Simple linear regression describes the average of an outcome, y, as a straight line in a predictor, x:

y = β0 + β1x + ε
β0, the intercept, is the average of y when x = 0; β1, the slope, is how much the average of y changes when x goes up by 1; ε is the scatter of each person around the line

The line is fitted by least squares: of all the straight lines, it is the one that makes the sum of the squared residuals (the vertical distances from the points to the line) as small as possible.

Fitting a line with lm()

lm() (“linear model”) takes a formula, outcome ~ predictor, and the data:

> fit <- lm(Pre.weight ~ Pre.height, data = fms)
> fit

Call:
lm(formula = Pre.weight ~ Pre.height, data = fms)

Coefficients:
(Intercept)   Pre.height
   -181.377        5.039

Genotypes as predictors

≈ 30 min

The same regression works for genotypes, once you decide how to turn a genotype into a predictor.

The additive model

A genetic association study asks whether an outcome changes with genotype. Coding the genotype as the number of copies of an allele (0, 1 or 2) makes it a numeric predictor, and the slope is the change in the outcome for each copy. This is the additive model:

Several predictors, confounding and interactions

≈ 45 min

With several predictors, each coefficient is an effect allowing for the others. That is how you deal with confounding, and how you ask whether an effect differs between groups.

Multiple regression

A model can have several predictors:

y = β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 + … + ε
each coefficient is the change in the average of y for a change of 1 in its predictor, with the other predictors held constant

A categorical predictor, such as Gender, becomes an indicator (or dummy) variable: R chooses a reference group (Female, which comes first alphabetically), and the coefficient GenderMale is the difference between men and women.

Confounding

A confounder is a variable that is associated with both the predictor you are interested in (the exposure) and the outcome, and isn’t on the causal path between them. It can create an association, or hide or distort one.

In chapter 1 you saw that people with a higher BMI gained less strength. But men have a higher BMI (25.0 on average, against 23.9 for women) and gained less, in percentage terms. How much of the BMI association is really an association with sex? Compare the BMI coefficient before and after adding sex to the model:

> lm(NDRM.CH ~ pre.BMI, data = fms)

Call:
lm(formula = NDRM.CH ~ pre.BMI, data = fms)

Coefficients:
(Intercept)      pre.BMI
     82.388       -1.224
> lm(NDRM.CH ~ pre.BMI + Gender, data = fms)

Call:
lm(formula = NDRM.CH ~ pre.BMI + Gender, data = fms)

Coefficients:
(Intercept)      pre.BMI   GenderMale
    84.9252      -0.9812     -20.5393

Allowing for sex, the BMI coefficient shrinks from −1.22 to −0.98 per unit of BMI: about a fifth of the crude association was due to sex. The rest remains, and could itself be confounded by things that weren’t measured. Adjusting can only allow for the confounders you have measured, and measured well.

Don’t adjust for everything

Adjust for confounders, not for variables that the exposure itself affects. If a genotype affected muscle mass, which in turn affected the gain in strength, adjusting for muscle mass would remove part of the very effect you want to measure. And with many predictors and few people, the estimates become unstable: as a rough guide, have at least 10 to 20 people (or, for a yes-or-no outcome, events) for each predictor.

Be careful with interactions. A test for an interaction has much less power than the test of the main effect, and it is easy to find one by chance if you look at enough subgroups. An interaction is most convincing when it was expected beforehand, as this one was, and when it is replicated.

Checking a model

≈ 20 min

A model is only as good as its assumptions. Look at its residuals before you trust its p-values.

Linear regression makes assumptions, which you can remember as LINE:

  • Linear: the average of the outcome changes in a straight line with each predictor;
  • Independent: the people (or samples) are independent of each other;
  • Normal: the residuals are roughly normal (this matters least, in large samples);
  • Equal variance: the scatter around the line is about the same everywhere.

Independence is the most important, and the hardest to check from the data: think about how the data were collected (repeated measurements of the same people, or relatives, aren’t independent). The others can be checked by looking at the residuals, the differences between the observed values and the fitted ones.

fitted() and residuals() take them out of a model:

R
diag <- tibble(fitted = fitted(fit), residual = residuals(fit))
ggplot(diag, aes(x = fitted, y = residual)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.4) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0)
R
ggplot(diag, aes(sample = residual)) +
  stat_qq() +
  stat_qq_line()

For weight and height, the residuals are a little skewed to the right (a few people are much heavier than their height predicts) and spread out a little more for taller people. With 1,032 people, the coefficients and their confidence intervals are still reliable, but a model of log(weight) would fit better. Base R draws similar plots with plot(fit).

Logistic regression and odds ratios

≈ 40 min

For a yes-or-no outcome, logistic regression models the log odds, and its exponentiated coefficients are odds ratios.

Yes-or-no outcomes

Many outcomes in medicine are binary: disease or not, response to treatment or not, case or control. A straight line is a poor model of a probability, because it goes below 0 and above 1. Logistic regression models the log odds instead, which can take any value:

log( p / (1 − p) ) = β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 + …
p is the probability of the outcome, and p / (1 − p) its odds; each coefficient is the change in the log odds for a change of 1 in its predictor

Because the coefficients are on the log scale, exponentiating one gives an odds ratio: the factor by which the odds are multiplied for a change of 1 in its predictor. In R, glm() (a generalised linear model) with family = binomial fits a logistic regression:

> m1 <- glm(obese ~ Gender, data = fms, family = binomial)
> coef(m1)
(Intercept)  GenderMale
 -0.9151340   0.5875292
> exp(coef(m1))
(Intercept)  GenderMale
   0.400463    1.799537

The odds of a BMI over 25 are 1.80 times as high in men as in women: exactly the odds ratio you calculated from the table last week. The linear regression of pre.BMI on esr1 gives p = 0.32, but the logistic regression of obese (BMI over 25) on esr1 gives p = 0.035. How could that happen, and what would you report?
They ask different questions: one about the mean BMI, the other about the proportion above 25, so they can disagree, especially when the evidence is weak. Turning a continuous outcome into yes or no throws away information, and choosing a cut-off after seeing the data is a form of p-hacking. Decide on the outcome and the model before the analysis, report the one you planned, and treat this weak, inconsistent evidence with caution.

What you have learnt this week, the formative homework, and how much of the practical you have done.

This week’s main ideas

  • Correlation measures how closely two variables follow a straight line; it isn’t causation, and it misses curved relationships.
  • Linear regression fits the line that minimises the squared residuals. The slope is the change in the average outcome per unit of the predictor; report it with its confidence interval. R² is the proportion of the variation explained.
  • A genotype becomes a predictor by counting alleles (the additive model), or as a category (the genotypic model).
  • In multiple regression, each coefficient allows for the other predictors. Adjusting for a confounder removes the part of an association that it explains; don’t adjust for variables on the causal path.
  • An interaction means that an effect differs between groups: here ACTN3 R577X’s effect differs between women and men.
  • Check the assumptions (LINE) with the residuals; independence matters most.
  • Logistic regression models the log odds of a yes-or-no outcome; exponentiated coefficients are odds ratios.
  • The tests of week 3 are linear models, which can also adjust for other variables.

What is one important limitation of a single-SNP regression like yours?
For example: the result is one of many possible tests (other SNPs, outcomes and codings), so a small p-value could be chance (week 5); the effect is small and explains little of the variation; there may be confounding by ancestry, which you haven’t adjusted for (week 5); the SNP may only be in linkage disequilibrium with the variant that matters; many participants weren’t genotyped, and if the missing data aren’t random the estimate could be biased; and an association needs replication in an independent study.
