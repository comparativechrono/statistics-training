This week’s data: the FAMuSS study

The FAMuSS study (Functional SNPs Associated with Muscle Size and Strength) asked why some people gain much more strength from exercise than others, and how much of the difference is genetic. About 1,400 young adults, aged 17 to 41, trained the biceps of their non-dominant arm (the one they don’t write with) twice a week for 12 weeks. The heaviest weight that each person could lift with that arm was measured before and after the training, and everyone was genotyped at a few hundred single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genes that were thought to affect muscle.

Describing data

≈ 45 min

Every analysis starts by describing the data: what kind of data each variable is, where its values are centred, how much they vary, and how many are missing.

What kind of data?

The first question to ask about any variable is what kind of data it is, because that decides how you summarise it, how you plot it and, next week, how you test it:

TypeWhat it isExamplesSummarised by
Continuousa measurement on a scale, which can take any value in a rangepre.BMI, NDRM.CH, gene expressionmean and SD, or median and IQR
Discrete (counts)whole numbers that count somethingthe number of copies of an allele (0, 1 or 2), reads at a basemean and SD, or the counts of each value
Categoricala set of named groups, in no orderGender, Race, a genotype such as CC, CT or TTthe count and proportion in each group
Ordinalgroups that have an order, but no fixed distance between themtumour grade, the stage of a diseasecounts, proportions and the median
Binarytwo groups: yes or noobese or not, case or control, mutation or notthe count and proportion of yeses

Genotypes are worth a second look. A genotype such as CT is a category, but you can also count the copies of an allele that a person carries: 0, 1 or 2 copies of the T allele. Both views are useful, and you will use both.

Centre and spread

A continuous variable is usually summarised by two numbers: one for its centre (a typical value) and one for its spread (how much the values vary).

  • The mean is the sum of the values divided by how many there are, written x̄. The median is the middle value when they are sorted: half the values are below it, and half above.
  • The standard deviation (SD) measures how far the values typically are from the mean:
s = √[ Σ (xi − x̄)² / (n − 1) ]
Square each value’s distance from the mean, add them up, divide by n − 1, and take the square root. (Dividing by n − 1 rather than n allows for having estimated the mean from the same data.)
  • The quartiles split the sorted values into four equal parts. The interquartile range (IQR) is the distance between the lower quartile (Q1, with 25% of the values below it) and the upper quartile (Q3, with 75% below it), so it covers the middle half of the data.

The mean and SD use every value, so a few extreme values can pull them a long way. The median and IQR don’t care how extreme the extreme values are, so they are robust. When a distribution is skewed (lopsided, with a long tail on one side) the mean is pulled towards the tail, and the median is usually the better summary of a typical value.

Why are the data missing?

Dropping missing values is only safe if the people who are missing are like the people who aren’t. If the people who dropped out of the training were mostly those who weren’t improving, the people left would make the training look better than it really was. Always ask why data are missing, and report how many are.

Seeing distributions

A summary in numbers can hide a lot. Before you go any further, look at the shape of each distribution.

Numbers such as the mean and SD summarise a distribution, but they can hide a lot: two variables with the same mean and SD can look completely different. Always look at the distribution itself. These are the plots you will use most:

Histograms

A histogram divides the range of values into bins and draws a bar for each bin, as tall as the number of values in it. The width of the bins (binwidth) is up to you: too wide and you lose the shape, too narrow and every bar is noise.

R
ggplot(fms, aes(x = NDRM.CH)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 10)

A box plot shows five things about a distribution, using the quartiles, so it isn’t pulled about by extreme values:

Probability distributions in genomics

≈ 45 min

Behind every statistical test is a model of how the data would vary by chance. Three distributions do most of the work in genomics: the normal, the binomial and the Poisson.

A probability distribution describes how a random quantity varies: which values it can take, and how likely each one is. A distribution for continuous data is a curve (a density) on which the area between two values is the probability of a value between them. A distribution for counts gives a probability to each whole number. Each distribution has parameters, such as its mean, that fix its exact shape.

Three distributions turn up again and again in genomics, and the statistical tests of the coming weeks are built on them.

The normal distribution

The normal distribution is the familiar bell-shaped curve. It has two parameters: its mean, μ, which is its centre, and its standard deviation, σ, which is its spread. Many measurements are roughly normal, such as height, or the log of a gene’s expression across people, because they add up many small, independent effects.

The distance of a value from the mean, in SDs, is its z-score: z = (x − μ) / σ. A z-score of 2 means “2 SDs above the mean”, whatever the units of the original measurement. The standard normal distribution has a mean of 0 and an SD of 1.

R has four functions for each distribution, whose names start with d, p, q or r:

PrefixExamplesWhat it gives you
ddnorm(x), dbinom(k, size, prob)the density at x (for a discrete distribution, the probability of exactly k)
ppnorm(x), pbinom(k, size, prob)the probability of a value less than or equal to x (or k)
qqnorm(p)the quantile: the value that a proportion p of the distribution is below
rrnorm(n), rbinom(n, size, prob), rpois(n, lambda)n random values from the distribution
> pnorm(1.96)
[1] 0.9750021
> qnorm(0.975)
[1] 1.959964
> 2 * pnorm(-1.96)
[1] 0.04999579

So 97.5% of a normal distribution is below 1.96 SDs above the mean, and a value is more than 1.96 SDs from the mean, in either direction, with a probability of 0.05. You will meet 1.96 and 0.05 many more times.

The binomial distribution

The binomial distribution counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same probability of success. Its parameters are the number of trials, n, and the probability, p. Its mean is np, and its variance is np(1 − p).

Genetics is full of binomial counts. Each person has two copies of each autosome, and if the copies are inherited independently, the number of copies of an allele that a person carries is binomial with n = 2 and p equal to the allele’s frequency. The probabilities of 0, 1 and 2 copies are (1 − p)², 2p(1 − p) and p²: the Hardy–Weinberg proportions, which you will test next week.

> dbinom(0:2, size = 2, prob = 0.3)
[1] 0.49 0.42 0.09

The same idea tells you about rare variants. A variant with a frequency of 1% is carried on 1 chromosome in 100. In a sample of 50 people (100 chromosomes), the chance of not seeing it at all is:

> dbinom(0, size = 100, prob = 0.01)
[1] 0.3660323

More than a third: a small study can easily miss a rare variant completely, which is why the studies of rare variants have to be large.

The Poisson distribution

The Poisson distribution counts events that happen independently, at a constant average rate, in a fixed space or time, when there is no fixed number of trials. Its single parameter, λ (lambda), is both its mean and its variance. Examples are the number of new (de novo) mutations in a child’s genome, or the number of sequencing reads that cover a base.

If a genome is sequenced to an average depth of 30×, the number of reads at each base is roughly Poisson with λ = 30. A variant at a base covered by fewer than 10 reads is hard to call, and the chance of that happening is small but not zero:

> ppois(9, lambda = 30)
[1] 7.121751e-06

About 7 in a million bases: in a genome of 3 billion bases, that is still about 20,000 bases.

When counts vary more than Poisson allows

In a Poisson distribution the variance equals the mean. Real counts often vary more than that: the coverage of a genome is uneven, and the read counts of a gene vary between people for biological reasons as well as by chance. This is called over-dispersion. The tools that analyse RNA-seq data, such as DESeq2 and edgeR, use the negative binomial distribution instead, which has a second parameter for the extra variation.

Samples, standard errors and confidence intervals

≈ 55 min

An estimate from a sample is never exactly right. The standard error and the confidence interval tell you how wrong it might be.

Samples and populations

The 1,397 people in FAMuSS are a sample from a much larger population: all the young adults who might train like this. What we want to know about is the population, such as the true mean BMI, or the true frequency of an allele. Such a quantity is a parameter, written with a Greek letter (μ for a mean). What we can calculate is an estimate from the sample (x̄). A different sample would give a different estimate, and the question is how different.

Seeing sampling variation

You can see sampling variation by simulation. Pretend, for a moment, that the 1,032 people with a BMI are the whole population, and draw small samples from them, as a small study would. pull() takes a single column out of a tibble, as a vector:

> bmi <- fms %>% drop_na(pre.BMI) %>% pull(pre.BMI)
> length(bmi)
[1] 1032
> set.seed(1)
> mean(sample(bmi, 30))
[1] 23.14553
> mean(sample(bmi, 30))
[1] 24.53907

Each sample of 30 people gives a different mean. replicate() repeats an expression many times, so you can draw 2,000 samples and keep the mean of each:

> means <- replicate(2000, mean(sample(bmi, 30)))

The 2,000 means form the sampling distribution of the mean. Plot them:

R
tibble(means = means) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = means)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.25)

Two things stand out. The means vary much less than the BMIs of individual people do. And their distribution is close to normal, even though BMI itself is skewed. That is the central limit theorem: means of reasonably large samples are close to normally distributed, whatever the shape of the data they come from. It is why the normal distribution underlies so many tests.

The standard error

The SD of a sampling distribution has a name of its own: the standard error (SE). For a mean, you don’t need a simulation to find it:

SE = s / √n
the SD of the data divided by the square root of the size of the sample
> sd(means)
[1] 0.7934721
> sd(bmi) / sqrt(30)
[1] 0.8358536

The simulation and the formula agree. Because of the √n, a sample four times as large has half the standard error. The SD and the SE are easy to confuse, but they answer different questions:

What it measuresWith more data, it…In R
Standard deviation (SD)how much the values vary from one person to anotherstays about the same as you collect more datasd(x)
Standard error (SE)how much an estimate, such as a mean, would vary from one sample to anothergets smaller as you collect more data (SD ÷ √n)sd(x) / sqrt(length(x))

For a proportion p̂, such as the frequency of an allele estimated from n chromosomes, the standard error is:

SE = √[ p̂ (1 − p̂) / n ]

Confidence intervals

A 95% confidence interval (CI) gives a range of plausible values for a parameter. Because the sampling distribution is close to normal, the estimate is within 1.96 standard errors of the true value in 95% of samples, so:

95% CI = estimate ± 1.96 × SE

For a mean, R uses a slightly larger number than 1.96 from the t distribution, qt(0.975, df = n − 1), which allows for having estimated the SD as well; for large samples the two are almost the same. t.test() calculates the interval for you, and $conf.int takes it out of the result:

> se <- sd(bmi) / sqrt(length(bmi))
> se
[1] 0.1425119
> mean(bmi) + c(-1, 1) * qt(0.975, df = length(bmi) - 1) * se
[1] 24.08302 24.64232
> t.test(bmi)$conf.int
[1] 24.08302 24.64232
attr(,"conf.level")
[1] 0.95

So, with 95% confidence, the mean BMI of the population these people come from is between 24.08 and 24.64. The box below says exactly what “95% confidence” means.

What “95% confident” means

The true mean is fixed: it is either inside this interval or not. The 95% describes the method: if you repeated the study many times, and calculated an interval in the same way each time, 95% of those intervals would contain the true value. It is not a 95% probability that the true value is in this particular interval. A wider interval means a less precise estimate: more data, or less variable data, give narrower intervals.

An allele’s frequency, with its confidence interval

The R577X variant of ACTN3 (the gene for α-actinin-3, a protein of fast muscle fibres) changes a codon for arginine (R, the C allele here) into a stop codon (X, the T allele). People with two copies of X make no α-actinin-3 at all. Each person contributes two alleles, so the frequency of the T allele is the number of T alleles divided by the number of alleles:

> g <- fms %>% drop_na(actn3_r577x) %>% pull(actn3_r577x)
> p_T <- (2 * sum(g == "TT") + sum(g == "CT")) / (2 * length(g))
> p_T
[1] 0.4897959
> p_T + c(-1, 1) * 1.96 * sqrt(p_T * (1 - p_T) / (2 * length(g)))
[1] 0.4642409 0.5153510

The bootstrap

Not every estimate has a simple formula for its standard error: the median doesn’t, for example. The bootstrap gets round this by treating the sample as if it were the population, and drawing new samples from it with replacement (so a person can be drawn more than once), each as large as the original. The spread of the estimates from those samples shows how much the estimate varies, and the middle 95% of them gives a confidence interval:

> ndrm <- fms %>% drop_na(NDRM.CH) %>% pull(NDRM.CH)
> set.seed(2)
> boot_medians <- replicate(2000, median(sample(ndrm, replace = TRUE)))
> quantile(boot_medians, c(0.025, 0.975))
 2.5% 97.5%
 42.9  50.0

So a 95% confidence interval for the median gain in strength is 42.9% to 50%. (NDRM.CH only takes some values, because the weights go up in steps of 2.5 pounds, so the bootstrapped medians do too.)

Your first Quarto report

≈ 30 min

Why a report in Quarto?

A result is only useful if someone else can see how you got it, and get it again. If you copy numbers from the Console into a Word document, nobody can check where they came from, and when the data change you have to do it all again by hand. A Quarto document (.qmd) keeps your text, your code and its results together. When you Render it, the code is run from the beginning and the results are put into a web page with your text. Your assessment for this course is a reproducible analysis of this kind, so each week’s report is practice for it.

The parts of a Quarto document

  • A header in YAML, between two lines of ---, with the document’s title, author and output format.
  • Text in Markdown: ## A heading, **bold**, *italic*, lists that start with -, and so on.
  • Chunks of R code, which start with ```{r} and end with ```. Lines at the top of a chunk that start with #| are its options: #| label: by-sex names it, #| echo: false hides the code but shows its results, and #| message: false and #| warning: false hide messages and warnings.
  • Inline code, `r …`, which puts the result of a little R into a sentence, so that the numbers in your text always match the data.
Render runs your document in a new R

When you render, the document is run from the top in a new R session, not in the R of the Console. It doesn’t see the objects you made in the Console, so it must load its packages and read its data itself. It also runs in the folder the document is saved in, your home folder here, so the data are in data/famuss.csv, wherever the Console’s working directory is. This is the whole point: if the document renders, everything it needs is in it.

This week’s main ideas

  • Decide what kind of data each variable is: continuous, a count, categorical, ordinal or binary. It decides the summary, the plot and the test.
  • Summarise a continuous variable with its centre and spread: the mean and SD, or, when it is skewed or has outliers, the median and IQR. Look at the distribution, and count the missing values.
  • The normal distribution describes measurements that add up many small effects; the binomial counts successes in a fixed number of trials, such as copies of an allele; the Poisson counts independent events, such as reads at a base; real counts are often over-dispersed.
  • An estimate varies from sample to sample. Its standard error measures how much, and shrinks with √n. A 95% confidence interval, about the estimate ± 1.96 SE, contains the true value in 95% of studies. The bootstrap gives an interval when there is no formula.
  • A Quarto report is run from the beginning, in a new R, every time it is rendered, so it shows exactly how each result was obtained.
