≈ 10 min

Genomic studies test thousands or millions of hypotheses at once. This week you will see what that does to p-values, how to correct for it, how ancestry can mislead, and how a genetic association study is done.

From one test to thousands

In weeks 3 and 4 you tested one hypothesis at a time, and a p-value below 0.05 was reasonable evidence against it. Genomics doesn’t work like that. A microarray measures more than 12,000 genes at once, and a genome-wide association study (GWAS) tests a million SNPs or more. If not one of a million SNPs had any effect, a threshold of 0.05 would still give you about 50,000 “significant” results. This week is about testing on that scale honestly: controlling false positives, recognising when ancestry creates associations that aren’t real, and putting it all together in a small association study.

Two functions for genotypes

This week you will work with the genotypes of many SNPs at once, so you need two small functions. hwe_p() is the Hardy–Weinberg test that you wrote in week 3, with one addition: it gives NA for a SNP that has only one allele. dosage() turns genotypes such as CC, CT and TT into the number of copies of the minor (less common) allele that each person carries, 0, 1 or 2: the additive coding of week 4, which it works out for any SNP. Create this file saves it in your home folder as snps.R and opens it in RStudio. Click Source to make the functions.

snps.R
# Two functions for genotypes such as "CC", "CT" and "TT" (week 5).

# The number of copies of the minor (less common) allele that each person
# carries: 0, 1 or 2 (NA if the person wasn't genotyped).
dosage <- function(g) {
  # all the alleles of the people who were genotyped, e.g. "C" "T" "C" "C" ...
  alleles <- unlist(strsplit(g[!is.na(g)], ""))
  # only one allele: everyone has 0 copies of the other one
  if (length(unique(alleles)) < 2) return(ifelse(is.na(g), NA, 0))
  # the less common allele
  minor <- names(sort(table(alleles)))[1]
  # the copies in the first and the second letter (TRUE counts as 1)
  (substr(g, 1, 1) == minor) + (substr(g, 2, 2) == minor)
}

# The p-value of a chi-square test of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium, as in week 3
# (NA if the SNP has only one allele).
hwe_p <- function(g) {
  g <- g[!is.na(g)]
  alleles <- unique(unlist(strsplit(g, "")))
  if (length(alleles) < 2) return(NA)
  n <- length(g)
  n_11 <- sum(g == paste0(alleles[1], alleles[1]))
  n_22 <- sum(g == paste0(alleles[2], alleles[2]))
  n_12 <- n - n_11 - n_22
  p <- (2 * n_11 + n_12) / (2 * n)
  expected <- n * c(p^2, 2 * p * (1 - p), (1 - p)^2)
  x2 <- sum((c(n_11, n_12, n_22) - expected)^2 / expected)
  pchisq(x2, df = 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
}

The multiple-testing problem

≈ 30 min

Each test at α = 0.05 has a 5% chance of a false positive. Do thousands of tests, and false positives are certain. First, see the problem.

When nothing is going on

Remember what α = 0.05 means (week 3): when the null hypothesis is true, a test gives p < 0.05 in 5% of studies, by chance. That is the price of one test. To see what it means for many tests, simulate 1,000 t-tests in which nothing is going on: each compares two groups of 10 values drawn from the same normal distribution. replicate() repeats the test 1,000 times, and set.seed() makes the random numbers the same as here:

> set.seed(1)
> null_p <- replicate(1000, t.test(rnorm(10), rnorm(10))$p.value)
> sum(null_p < 0.05)
[1] 50

Fifty of the 1,000 tests are “significant”, just as α promises, and every one of them is a false positive. Draw a histogram of the p-values, with a bar for each interval of 0.05:

R
tibble(p = null_p) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = p)) +
  geom_histogram(breaks = seq(0, 1, by = 0.05))

The bars are all about the same height. When the null hypothesis is true, p-values are spread evenly between 0 and 1: they follow a uniform distribution, so a p-value below 0.05 is no more likely than one between 0.50 and 0.55. That is exactly why 5% of them fall below 0.05.

The family-wise error rate

The family-wise error rate (FWER) is the probability of at least one false positive among a whole family of tests. For m independent tests of true null hypotheses, each at level α:

FWER = 1 − (1 − α)m
with α = 0.05: 5% for one test, 64% for 20 tests, and practically 100% for 12,000

Testing every position of a protein

HIV mutates quickly, and mutations in its protease, the enzyme that protease inhibitors block, can make the virus resistant to these drugs. The Virco data have, for 1,066 samples of HIV-1 (nearly all from patients), the amino acid at each of the 99 positions of the protease (the columns P1 to P99) and how resistant each virus is to several drugs. IDV.Fold and NFV.Fold are the fold resistance to two protease inhibitors, indinavir and nelfinavir: how many times as much drug it takes to inhibit this virus as a reference virus.

Do some mutations affect one drug more than the other? As the trait, take the difference between the two:

Now test each of them. apply(mut, 2, …) applies a function to each column of the matrix (2 means the columns; 1 would mean the rows). > p_virco <- apply(mut, 2, function(m) t.test(trait[m], trait[!m])$p.value)

Correcting for many tests

≈ 40 min

Two ways of correcting for many tests: Bonferroni, which guards against any false positive, and the false discovery rate, which keeps the proportion of false positives small.

Bonferroni: controlling the family-wise error rate

The simplest correction is Bonferroni’s. To keep the family-wise error rate at α across m tests, test each one at α / m. For the 47 positions, that is 0.05 / 47 = 0.00106. Equivalently, multiply each p-value by m (capping the result at 1) and compare it with α. p.adjust() does this:

Bonferroni: reject when p ≤ α / m, or when the adjusted p-value, min(1, m × p), ≤ α
> 0.05 / length(p_virco)
[1] 0.00106383
> p_bonf <- p.adjust(p_virco, method = "bonferroni")
> sum(p_bonf < 0.05)
[1] 8
> names(p_virco)[p_bonf < 0.05]
[1] "P11" "P30" "P48" "P55" "P76" "P82" "P88" "P89"

Eight positions survive. Bonferroni guarantees that the chance of even one false positive among them is at most 5%, whatever the tests (independent or not). That guarantee is expensive: with many tests the threshold becomes so strict that real effects are missed, so the tests have little power. It is also conservative when the tests are correlated, as they are for SNPs close together on a chromosome (chapter 5): it treats each test as a separate chance of a false positive, when correlated tests tend to be wrong together.

The false discovery rate

Often you don’t need to be sure that every finding is real. If you are choosing genes to follow up in the lab, a few false leads in the list are fine, as long as most of the list is right. The false discovery rate (FDR) is the expected proportion of false positives among the results you call significant (your “discoveries”). Controlling the FDR at 5% means that, on average, no more than 5% of your discoveries are false.

The Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure controls the FDR at a level q:

  1. Sort the m p-values from smallest to largest: p(1) ≤ p(2) ≤ … ≤ p(m).
  2. Find the largest rank i for which p(i) ≤ (i / m) × q.
  3. Call the i smallest p-values significant.

The smallest p-value is compared with q / m, as in Bonferroni, but each next one with a less strict threshold. p.adjust(…, method = "BH") gives BH-adjusted p-values, which you compare with q. A test’s adjusted p-value is the smallest FDR at which it would count as a discovery: the idea behind the q-value.

> p_bh <- p.adjust(p_virco, method = "BH")
> sum(p_bh < 0.05)
[1] 18

BH keeps 18 positions, against Bonferroni’s 8. Put the results in a table, sorted by p-value:

+                         bonferroni = p_bonf, bh = p_bh) %>%

The top of the list makes sense biologically. Statistics found the known biology among 47 positions; the corrections tell you how far down the list to trust it.

Two common misunderstandings

“Bonferroni is always right.” It is always safe against false positives, but with many correlated tests it is far stricter than it needs to be, and it misses real effects. Genome-wide studies allow for the correlation between SNPs (chapter 3).

“An FDR of 5% means that each discovery has a 5% chance of being false.” The FDR is about the list as a whole: of the 18 positions, you expect about 1 to be false. Those at the top of the list (P30, with p = 4 × 10−12) are almost certainly real; those near the cut-off are much less certain.

In two sentences, say what the family-wise error rate and the false discovery rate each control, and when you would use each.
The FWER is the probability of at least one false positive among all the tests; controlling it (with Bonferroni) suits confirmatory claims, such as a variant that affects a disease, where any false positive matters. The FDR is the expected proportion of false positives among the results called significant; controlling it (with Benjamini–Hochberg) suits screening, where a few false leads in a list of candidates are acceptable in exchange for more power.

The probes that pass BH form the two arms of the “volcano”. Some have large differences but weak evidence (few patients, or a lot of variation), and some have strong evidence for tiny differences. A difference worth following up in the lab usually needs both: strong evidence and a size that could matter biologically.

Genome-wide significance

A GWAS tests a million or more SNPs, and the threshold it uses, p < 5 × 10−8, is a Bonferroni correction. Because of linkage disequilibrium (chapter 5), the millions of common SNPs in a European-ancestry genome behave like about one million independent tests, and 0.05 / 1,000,000 = 5 × 10−8. So the threshold counts independent regions of the genome rather than SNPs, which makes it less conservative than a Bonferroni correction for every SNP. It is a convention, not a law: studies of African-ancestry populations, whose genomes have shorter stretches of LD and so more independent tests, and studies of rare variants, may need stricter thresholds.

The winner’s curse, and replication

A SNP that only just passes a strict threshold is likely to have had some luck: its effect, in that sample, happened to be estimated on the high side. So the first estimate of the effect of a newly discovered variant is usually too large, and it shrinks in later studies. This is the winner’s curse. The answer is replication: a finding is accepted when an independent study, ideally of a different population, finds the same association, and the effect is best estimated in the replication study.

How ancestry creates false associations

Now suppose that a study mixes people from two populations, and that the trait differs between the populations for reasons that have nothing to do with a particular SNP: diet, environment, other genes, or even how the samples were collected. Then every SNP whose allele frequency differs between the populations will seem to be associated with the trait. This is population stratification, and it is a form of confounding (week 4): ancestry affects both the genotype and the trait.

To see what this does to a genome-wide study, simulate one. The code below makes the genotypes of 400 people at 500 SNPs, using what you know from week 2: the number of copies of an allele that a person carries is binomial, with size 2 and the allele’s frequency as its probability. Half of the people come from population 1 and half from population 2, whose allele frequencies differ a little. The trait is higher, on average, in population 2, and no SNP has any effect on it:

R
set.seed(42)
# the allele frequencies of 500 SNPs in population 1
f1 <- runif(500, min = 0.1, max = 0.9)
# in population 2 they differ a little (but stay between 0.02 and 0.98)
f2 <- pmin(pmax(f1 + rnorm(500, mean = 0, sd = 0.1), 0.02), 0.98)
# the genotypes (0, 1 or 2 copies) of 200 people from each population
sim_geno <- rbind(sapply(f1, function(f) rbinom(200, size = 2, prob = f)),
                  sapply(f2, function(f) rbinom(200, size = 2, prob = f)))
sim_pop <- rep(c("pop1", "pop2"), each = 200)
# the trait: higher in population 2, on average; no SNP has any effect on it
sim_trait <- rnorm(400, mean = ifelse(sim_pop == "pop2", 1, 0))

sapply() applies a function to each element of a vector, here each of the 500 frequencies, and puts the results together: 500 columns of 200 genotypes. rbind() stacks the two populations, so sim_geno is a matrix with a row for each of the 400 people and a column for each SNP.

Principal component analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) finds the directions in which a set of variables varies most. The first principal component (PC1) is the combination of the variables that varies most from person to person, PC2 the one that varies most of what is left, and so on. Applied to genotypes, the first principal components usually capture ancestry, because ancestry affects many SNPs at once. prcomp() does it. scale. = TRUE puts every SNP on the same scale first; otherwise SNPs whose genotypes vary more would count for more.

> pca <- prcomp(sim_geno, scale. = TRUE)
> summary(pca)$importance[, 1:4]
                            PC1      PC2      PC3      PC4
Standard deviation     3.814792 2.126851 2.085455 2.053172
Proportion of Variance 0.029110 0.009050 0.008700 0.008430
Cumulative Proportion  0.029110 0.038150 0.046850 0.055280

PC1 explains only 2.9% of the variation in the genotypes, and PC2 to PC4 about 0.9% each. But plot PC1 and PC2:

R
pcs <- tibble(population = sim_pop, PC1 = pca$x[, 1], PC2 = pca$x[, 2])
ggplot(pcs, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2, colour = population)) +
  geom_point()

PC1 separates the two populations completely. It has found every person’s ancestry from their genotypes alone, without being told who came from where. (The sign of a principal component is arbitrary, so your plot could be the mirror image of someone else’s.)

False positives, and how to remove them

Test every SNP for an association with the trait, first on its own and then adjusted for PC1, as a covariate in the regression. Each line takes a few seconds:

> p_naive <- apply(sim_geno, 2, function(g)
+   summary(lm(sim_trait ~ g))$coefficients[2, 4])
> p_pc1 <- apply(sim_geno, 2, function(g)
+   summary(lm(sim_trait ~ g + pcs$PC1))$coefficients[2, 4])
> sum(p_naive < 0.05)
[1] 148
> sum(p_pc1 < 0.05)
[1] 25
> sum(p.adjust(p_naive, method = "bonferroni") < 0.05)
[1] 10

Without adjustment, 148 of the 500 SNPs have p < 0.05, against the 25 expected by chance, and 10 even pass the Bonferroni correction: ten findings that would be published as significant, and every one of them false. Adjusted for PC1, 25 SNPs have p < 0.05: just what chance predicts.

A QQ plot (quantile–quantile plot) of p-values shows this at a glance. It plots the p-values you observed, sorted and on the −log10 scale, against the values you would expect if every null hypothesis were true. ppoints(500) gives those expected values: 500 evenly spaced probabilities between 0 and 1.

R
qq <- tibble(expected = -log10(ppoints(500)),
             unadjusted = -log10(sort(p_naive)),
             adjusted = -log10(sort(p_pc1))) %>%
  pivot_longer(c(unadjusted, adjusted),
               names_to = "model", values_to = "observed")
ggplot(qq, aes(x = expected, y = observed, colour = model)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_abline()

If nothing is going on, the points lie on the diagonal. The unadjusted p-values lift off it from the very start: the whole distribution is shifted, which is the signature of inflation by something that affects every test, such as population structure. A few real associations would only lift the last few points, at the top right. After adjustment for PC1, the points follow the line.

Population structure in real studies

A GWAS calculates principal components from many thousands of SNPs across the genome, after removing SNPs in strong LD with each other so that a few regions don’t dominate. It includes the first few (often 10) as covariates, or uses a linear mixed model, which also allows for relatedness between participants. Self-reported ethnicity is a much cruder measure of ancestry. Studies also report the genomic inflation factor, λ, which compares the middle (median) of the observed test statistics with what is expected if nothing is going on. λ near 1 means no inflation; above about 1.1 is a warning sign.

Principal components are not races

A PCA of real people often shows clusters, but they are clusters of people with similar ancestry along axes that are continuous: many people fall between clusters, and the clusters depend on which populations were sampled. Read a PCA plot as a map of genetic similarity, not as evidence of discrete groups.

Linkage disequilibrium

≈ 20 min

Nearby variants travel together. That makes genotyping arrays possible, and it shapes how the results of association studies look and what they mean.

Alleles at SNPs that are close together on a chromosome tend to be inherited together, because recombination rarely separates them. So, in a population, a person’s allele at one SNP tells you something about their allele at a SNP nearby. This association between the alleles of nearby SNPs is linkage disequilibrium (LD).

LD is usually measured by r², the square of the correlation between the numbers of copies of the alleles at two SNPs: 1 means that one SNP predicts the other perfectly, and 0 that they are independent. (Strictly, r² is defined for the alleles that sit together on one copy of a chromosome; with genotypes, the correlation between the allele counts is a close and widely used approximation.) The four SNPs in the HGDP data lie within a few thousand bases of each other in AKT1. across(everything(), dosage) applies dosage() (from snps.R) to every column (you met across() in week 2), and cor() gives the correlations between all the columns:

What LD means for association studies

  • Tag SNPs. Because of LD, a genotyping array doesn’t need to measure every variant: a few hundred thousand well-chosen SNPs “tag” most of the common variants in the genome, and many others can be filled in statistically (imputation).
  • Peaks, not points. A variant that affects a trait makes every SNP in LD with it look associated too, which is why an association in a GWAS shows up as a tower of SNPs in a Manhattan plot.
  • Association is not causation. The SNP with the smallest p-value may just be in LD with the variant that matters. Finding that variant needs fine-mapping and laboratory studies.
  • Correlated tests. Tests of SNPs in LD are correlated, so there are fewer independent tests than SNPs: Bonferroni is too strict, and genome-wide significance counts independent regions instead.
  • LD differs between populations. It extends over shorter distances in African populations, which have the longest history, so a SNP that tags a variant in one population may not tag it in another.

Quality control

Three standard checks for each SNP:

  • Its call rate: the proportion of people with a genotype. A SNP that often fails may be genotyped badly. A GWAS removes SNPs with a call rate below 95–98%, but FAMuSS genotyped many of its SNPs in only part of the study, so here the threshold is 30%, which removes the SNPs genotyped in very few people.
  • Its minor allele frequency (MAF): the frequency of the less common allele. A rare variant gives unstable estimates and has very little power in a study of this size, so remove SNPs with a MAF below 5%.
  • Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium: a strong departure is often a sign of genotyping errors (week 3). Test it within one ancestry group (here the Caucasian participants, the largest group), and remove SNPs with p < 10−6.

What does it show?

No SNP is significant once you allow for 163 tests. The smallest p-value is for acdc_rs1501299, a SNP in the gene for adiponectin, a hormone made by fat tissue: each copy of its A allele went with a gain 4.3 points smaller (p = 0.007). ACTN3 R577X comes second.

In week 4 you could reasonably take p = 0.013 for ACTN3 as evidence of an effect. That was a single test of a hypothesis that the study had been designed to test, with support from earlier research. Here, the same p-value is one of 163, and the second smallest of 163 p-values is expected to be about this small by chance alone. How much a p-value means depends on how many tests were done, and on how plausible the hypothesis was beforehand.

The study is also small for genetics: 400 to 975 people were tested for each SNP, and the effects of common variants on complex traits are small (ACTN3 explained about 1% of the variation in the gain in strength). Genome-wide studies of such traits need tens or hundreds of thousands of people, and their findings need replication.

Summary and homework

What you have learnt this week, the homework that completes your mini-GWAS, and how much of the practical you have done.

This week’s main ideas

  • When the null hypothesis is true, p-values are uniform: 5% of tests give p < 0.05 by chance, so many tests give many false positives.
  • The family-wise error rate is the chance of at least one false positive; Bonferroni controls it by testing at α / m. It is safe but conservative, especially for correlated tests.
  • The false discovery rate is the expected proportion of false positives among the discoveries; Benjamini–Hochberg controls it, with more power. Use it for screening; use FWER control to confirm.
  • A histogram of p-values that is flat with a spike near 0 shows real signals on a background of true nulls.
  • Genome-wide significance, 5 × 10−8, is a Bonferroni correction for about a million independent common variants. First estimates of effects are inflated (the winner’s curse), so findings need replication.
  • Population stratification confounds association studies when ancestry is related to both genotype and trait. Principal components of the genotypes capture ancestry and are used as covariates; QQ plots and λ show inflation.
  • Linkage disequilibrium makes nearby SNPs travel together: GWAS hits are peaks, the top SNP may not be causal, and tests are correlated.
  • A GWAS goes: quality control (call rate, MAF, HWE), a test of every SNP with covariates, a correction for the number of tests, QQ and Manhattan plots, and an interpretation that considers LD, confounding and replication.

What would your QQ plot look like if the analysis were confounded by population structure that you hadn’t allowed for, and how would you fix it?
The points would rise above the diagonal from early on, across the whole range of p-values, not just at the top (λ above 1), because ancestry would shift the test statistics of every SNP whose frequency differs between the groups. You would allow for ancestry: with principal components of genome-wide genotypes as covariates (or a mixed model), or by analysing a group of similar ancestry on its own, and then check that the QQ plot and λ come back to the line.
