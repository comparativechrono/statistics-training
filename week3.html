This week’s new data: acute lymphoblastic leukaemia

Acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL) is a cancer of the cells that would become lymphocytes. It is not one disease: different genetic changes drive it, and they matter for treatment. In about a quarter of adults, the leukaemic cells carry the Philadelphia chromosome, a translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22 that fuses the genes BCR and ABL1. The fusion protein, BCR-ABL1, is a tyrosine kinase that is always switched on, and it is the target of drugs such as imatinib. Another group has a translocation between chromosomes 4 and 11 that fuses KMT2A (also called MLL, or ALL1) to AFF1 (AF4).

The ALL study (Chiaretti and others, 2004) measured the expression of about 12,600 genes, with microarrays, in leukaemic cells from adults with ALL. leukaemia.csv has 89 of its patients with B-cell ALL, with one row for each patient:

The logic of a hypothesis test

≈ 40 min

A hypothesis test asks one question: if there were really no difference, how surprising would the data be? Build the answer yourself, by shuffling labels.

The question

Is ABL1 expressed differently in BCR/ABL leukaemias and in NEG leukaemias? Start by looking:

> two <- leuk %>% filter(mol.biol %in% c("BCR/ABL", "NEG"))
> two %>% group_by(mol.biol) %>% summarise(n = n(), mean = mean(ABL1), sd = sd(ABL1))
# A tibble: 2 × 4
  mol.biol     n  mean    sd
  <chr>    <int> <dbl> <dbl>
1 BCR/ABL     37  9.78 0.586
2 NEG         42  8.68 0.468
R
ggplot(two, aes(x = mol.biol, y = ABL1)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.1, alpha = 0.5)

The mean in the BCR/ABL group is about 1.1 higher, on a log2 scale: a little over twice as much ABL1 RNA. But the values in each group vary, and with only 37 and 42 patients you would expect the two means to differ a bit even if, in all such patients, they were the same. How can you tell a real difference from chance?

The logic of a hypothesis test

  1. State a null hypothesis, H0: the thing you would like to disprove. Here, “the mean expression of ABL1 is the same in both subtypes.” The alternative hypothesis, H1, is that it isn’t.
  2. Choose a test statistic: a number that measures how far the data are from what H0 says. Here, the difference between the two means.
  3. Work out the null distribution: the values the statistic would take, by chance, if H0 were true.
  4. Compare. The p-value is the probability, if H0 were true, of a statistic at least as extreme as the one you observed. A small p-value means the data would be surprising if H0 were true.

A null distribution, by shuffling

If the subtype made no difference to ABL1, the labels BCR/ABL and NEG would just be names attached at random to the 79 values. So shuffle them: give the same values random labels, and calculate the difference between the means again. Each shuffle gives one value of the statistic as it would be if H0 were true. This is a permutation test, and it needs no formulas at all.

You will calculate the difference many times, so write a small function for it. function(y, group) makes a function with two arguments, and the last line inside the braces is the result it gives back:

> diff_means <- function(y, group) {
+   mean(y[group == "BCR/ABL"]) - mean(y[group == "NEG"])
+ }
> obs <- diff_means(two$ABL1, two$mol.biol)
> obs
[1] 1.09962

sample(), given a vector, shuffles it. Two shuffles give two differences that arise by chance alone:

> set.seed(1)
> diff_means(two$ABL1, sample(two$mol.biol))
[1] 0.2948777
> diff_means(two$ABL1, sample(two$mol.biol))
[1] 0.1926963

Now do it 5,000 times, and see how often chance alone gives a difference as large as the one you observed (in either direction, so compare the absolute values, with abs()):

> set.seed(1)
> null <- replicate(5000, diff_means(two$ABL1, sample(two$mol.biol)))
> max(abs(null))
[1] 0.6486486
> mean(abs(null) >= abs(obs))
[1] 0

In 5,000 shuffles, the largest difference was 0.65, and none was as large as 1.10. So the p-value is less than 1 in 5,000 (p < 0.0002): if the subtype made no difference, a difference this large would be very surprising. You can reject the null hypothesis.

R
tibble(null = null) %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = null)) +
  geom_histogram(binwidth = 0.02) +
  geom_vline(xintercept = obs, colour = "red")

Decisions and errors

To turn a p-value into a decision, you choose a significance level, α, beforehand, usually 0.05, and reject H0 when p ≤ α. Either way, you can be wrong:

You reject the null hypothesis (p ≤ α)You don’t reject it (p > α)
The null hypothesis is true (no real difference)a false positive: a type I error. Its probability is α, which you choose (usually 0.05)right
The null hypothesis is false (a real difference)right: the probability of this is the power of the testa false negative: a type II error. Its probability is β, and power = 1 − β

The power of a test is the chance of detecting a difference when there really is one. It grows with the size of the sample and the size of the difference, and it shrinks as the data become more variable. A small study can easily miss a real difference: a large p-value doesn’t show that there is no difference.

What a p-value is not
  • It is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true. It is calculated assuming that H0 is true.
  • It is not a measure of how large or important a difference is. A tiny difference in a huge study can have a tiny p-value. Always report the size of the difference, with a confidence interval.
  • p > 0.05 does not show that there is no difference: “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence”.
  • 0.05 is a convention, not a law of nature: p = 0.049 and p = 0.051 are practically the same evidence.

Comparing two groups

≈ 40 min

The t-test is the most used test in medical research. Learn what it calculates, what it assumes, what to use when those assumptions fail, and how to handle measurements made twice on the same people.

The t-test

Shuffling works for any statistic, but for a difference between two means there is a quicker way. If the means are approximately normally distributed (which, thanks to the central limit theorem, they usually are), the difference divided by its standard error follows a known distribution, the t distribution:

t = (x̄1 − x̄2) / SE,   where SE = √( s1² / n1 + s2² / n2 )
how many standard errors apart the two means are

A t of 0 means the means are equal; the further t is from 0, in either direction, the stronger the evidence against H0. The t distribution looks like the normal distribution, but with heavier tails when the samples are small; its shape is set by the degrees of freedom (df). R’s t.test() does the whole calculation. With a formula, ABL1 ~ mol.biol, it compares ABL1 between the groups of mol.biol:

> t.test(ABL1 ~ mol.biol, data = two)

	Welch Two Sample t-test

data:  ABL1 by mol.biol
t = 9.1326, df = 68.791, p-value = 1.759e-13
alternative hypothesis: true difference in means between group BCR/ABL and group NEG is not equal to 0
95 percent confidence interval:
 0.859405 1.339836
sample estimates:
mean in group BCR/ABL     mean in group NEG
             9.780811              8.681190

Read it from the bottom. The means are 9.78 and 8.68, so the difference is 1.10. The 95% confidence interval for the difference is 0.86 to 1.34: this is the most useful line, because it tells you how large the difference is likely to be. t = 9.13 means the means are more than 9 standard errors apart, and p = 1.8 × 10−13 agrees with the permutation test. R uses Welch’s t-test by default, which doesn’t assume that the two groups have the same SD (hence the df of 68.8, which isn’t a whole number). var.equal = TRUE gives the original Student’s t-test, which does.

Assumptions

Every test makes assumptions, and its p-value is only right when they are (roughly) true. The t-test assumes that:

  • the observations are independent: each patient is counted once, and patients don’t influence each other;
  • each group is roughly normal, or the groups are large enough for the central limit theorem to make the means normal. Outliers and strong skew matter most in small samples.

A QQ plot (quantile–quantile plot) checks normality. It plots the sorted values against the values you would expect from a normal distribution: if the data are normal, the points lie close to a straight line. A curve means skew, and points that peel away at the ends mean heavy tails or outliers.

R
ggplot(two, aes(sample = ABL1)) +
  stat_qq() +
  stat_qq_line() +
  facet_wrap(~ mol.biol)

When the assumptions fail: the Wilcoxon test

The Wilcoxon rank-sum test (also called the Mann–Whitney test) replaces the values with their ranks (1 for the smallest, 2 for the next, and so on) and asks whether one group’s ranks tend to be higher. It doesn’t assume normality and isn’t affected by outliers, so it suits skewed data and small samples. The price is a little less power when the data really are normal, and its result is about the ranks rather than about a difference in means.

> wilcox.test(ABL1 ~ mol.biol, data = two)

	Wilcoxon rank sum exact test

data:  ABL1 by mol.biol
W = 1430.5, p-value = 9.492e-13
alternative hypothesis: true location shift is not equal to 0

Paired data

The FAMuSS participants were measured before and after the training. These measurements are paired: each “after” belongs with a “before” from the same person, and people who are strong before tend to be strong after. A paired t-test tests whether the mean of the differences is zero, which removes the variation between people and makes the test much more powerful:

> t.test(fms$Post_NDRM_Max, fms$Pre_NDRM_Max, paired = TRUE)

	Paired t-test

Comparing several groups

≈ 40 min

With three or more groups, start with one test of whether the groups differ at all, and only then ask which ones differ.

Why not lots of t-tests?

With three subtypes there are three pairs of groups to compare. If you did a t-test for each, at α = 0.05, the chance of at least one false positive would be 1 − 0.953, about 14%, not 5%. The more groups, the worse it gets. Instead, start with one test of the question “do the means differ at all?”

Analysis of variance

One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) compares the variation between the groups’ means with the variation within the groups. If the groups don’t really differ, the means should be about as variable as chance makes them, given the scatter within each group.

F = (variation between the groups, per degree of freedom) / (variation within the groups, per degree of freedom)
If H0 is true (all the means are equal), F is about 1. The larger F is, the stronger the evidence that at least one mean differs.

Here is HOXA9, a gene that controls the development of blood cells, in the three subtypes:

Which groups differ?

A significant ANOVA tells you that the means aren’t all equal, but not which ones differ. A post hoc test compares every pair of groups, and adjusts for the number of comparisons so that the chance of any false positive stays at 5%. Tukey’s honest significant difference is the usual one:

When the assumptions fail: Kruskal–Wallis

The Kruskal–Wallis test is the rank-based version of ANOVA, just as the Wilcoxon test is of the t-test. Use it when the groups are skewed or have outliers, especially when they are small (the ALL1/AF4 group has only 10 patients):

Categorical data and genotypes

≈ 45 min

Counts need tests of their own: the chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, the odds ratio to measure association, and the Hardy–Weinberg test, which checks the quality of genotypes.

Tables of counts

When both variables are categorical, the data are counts, and they are best shown as a contingency table. Did the patients with BCR/ABL leukaemia do worse after their first treatment? table() counts each combination of subtype and response (and leaves out the patients whose response is missing):

> tab <- table(two$mol.biol, two$remission)
> tab

          CR REF
  BCR/ABL 22   8
  NEG     38   1

8 of the 30 BCR/ABL patients (27%) didn’t go into remission (REF), against 1 of the 39 NEG patients (3%).

The chi-square test

If remission didn’t depend on the subtype, each subtype would have the same proportion of REF patients as the table as a whole, 9 of 69. The expected count of each cell is what it would hold if that were so:

expected count = (row total × column total) / grand total

The chi-square (χ²) statistic adds up how far the observed counts (O) are from the expected counts (E):

χ² = Σ (O − E)² / E
with (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) degrees of freedom: 1 for a 2 × 2 table
> ct <- chisq.test(tab)
Warning message:
In chisq.test(tab) : Chi-squared approximation may be incorrect
> ct

	Pearson's Chi-squared test with Yates' continuity correction

data:  tab
X-squared = 6.6899, df = 1, p-value = 0.009696
> ct$expected

                CR      REF
  BCR/ABL 26.08696 3.913043
  NEG     33.91304 5.086957

R warns that the chi-square approximation may be incorrect. The test relies on a large-sample approximation, which needs every expected count to be at least about 5, and two of them are less than that. (For a 2 × 2 table, R also applies Yates’ continuity correction, which makes the test a little more cautious.)

Fisher’s exact test, and the odds ratio

Fisher’s exact test calculates the p-value exactly, from all the tables with the same totals, so it is right however small the counts are. Use it whenever expected counts are small:

> fisher.test(tab)

	Fisher's Exact Test for Count Data

data:  tab
p-value = 0.00802

95 percent confidence interval:
 0.001595656 0.619891879
sample estimates:
odds ratio
0.07492174

Besides the p-value (0.008), Fisher’s test gives the odds ratio, a measure of how strongly the two variables are associated. The odds of something is the probability that it happens divided by the probability that it doesn’t: 22 of the BCR/ABL patients went into remission and 8 didn’t, so their odds of remission were 22 / 8 = 2.75. For the NEG patients they were 38 / 1 = 38.

odds ratio = odds in group 1 / odds in group 2 = (22 / 8) / (38 / 1) = 0.072
Fisher’s test gives 0.075, a slightly different kind of estimate. An odds ratio of 1 means no association.

So the BCR/ABL patients had less than a tenth of the odds of remission of the NEG patients: equally, their odds of being refractory were more than ten times as high. Which way round an odds ratio is depends on the order of the rows and columns, so always say what it compares. Its confidence interval (0.002 to 0.62) is very wide, because only 9 patients were refractory.

Odds ratios are everywhere in genetic epidemiology: case–control studies and GWAS report the effect of an allele as an odds ratio. Next week you will estimate them with logistic regression, which can also adjust for other variables.

Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

Last week you saw that, if the two copies of a gene are inherited independently, the genotypes of a SNP with allele frequencies p and q = 1 − p should be in the proportions p², 2pq and q². This is Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (HWE). It holds when mating is random and there is no selection, migration or mutation to disturb it. Testing it is a standard quality control step for genotyping data, because the commonest reason for a SNP to fail is a problem with the genotyping, such as heterozygotes that are called as homozygotes.

The test is a chi-square test of the observed genotype counts against the counts expected from the allele frequency. It has 1 degree of freedom: 3 genotypes, minus 1 because the counts must add up to the total, minus 1 more because the allele frequency was estimated from the same data. Here it is for ACTN3 R577X, step by step:

> geno <- fms %>% drop_na(actn3_r577x) %>% count(actn3_r577x)
> geno
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  actn3_r577x     n
  <chr>       <int>
1 CC            216
2 CT            318
3 TT            201
> n <- sum(geno$n)
> p_C <- (2 * geno$n[1] + geno$n[2]) / (2 * n)
> p_C
[1] 0.5102041
> expected <- n * c(p_C^2, 2 * p_C * (1 - p_C), (1 - p_C)^2)
> expected
[1] 191.3265 367.3469 176.3265
> x2 <- sum((geno$n - expected)^2 / expected)
> x2
[1] 13.2634
> pchisq(x2, df = 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
[1] 0.0002706381

There are fewer heterozygotes than expected (318 against 367), and p = 0.0003. To test other SNPs, wrap the steps in a function that takes a vector of genotypes, works out which two letters are the alleles, and gives back the p-value. You will use this function again in week 5:

hwe.R
hwe_p <- function(g) {
  g <- g[!is.na(g)]                         # the people who were genotyped
  alleles <- unique(unlist(strsplit(g, "")))  # the two letters, e.g. "C" and "T"
  n <- length(g)
  n_11 <- sum(g == paste0(alleles[1], alleles[1]))   # e.g. CC
  n_22 <- sum(g == paste0(alleles[2], alleles[2]))   # e.g. TT
  n_12 <- n - n_11 - n_22                             # the heterozygotes
  p <- (2 * n_11 + n_12) / (2 * n)                    # the frequency of the first allele
  expected <- n * c(p^2, 2 * p * (1 - p), (1 - p)^2)
  x2 <- sum((c(n_11, n_12, n_22) - expected)^2 / expected)
  pchisq(x2, df = 1, lower.tail = FALSE)
}
Create the file and click Source in RStudio to make the function, or copy it into the Console.
> hwe_p(fms$actn3_r577x)
[1] 0.0002706381
> fms %>% filter(Race == "Caucasian") %>% pull(actn3_r577x) %>% hwe_p()
[1] 0.07598464

In everyone, the SNP fails the test; in the Caucasian participants alone, it doesn’t (p = 0.08). FAMuSS mixes people of several ancestries, in whom the allele frequencies differ. Each group can be in equilibrium on its own, but pooling groups with different allele frequencies always gives too few heterozygotes (the Wahlund effect). So test HWE within groups of similar ancestry, and treat a failure as a warning to investigate rather than as a finding.

HWE in genome-wide studies

Before a genome-wide association study, SNPs that fail HWE badly (for example p < 10−6, tested in the controls) are usually removed, because genotyping errors can create false associations. The threshold is strict because a study tests hundreds of thousands of SNPs, so some will fail by chance (week 5). Testing in controls avoids removing a real association: in cases, a SNP that causes the disease may well be out of equilibrium.

Choosing the right test

≈ 20 min

Which test you use depends on the kind of data, the design of the study and the assumptions you can make. Practise choosing.

Choosing a test comes down to three questions. What kind of outcome is it: continuous, or counts in categories? How many groups are you comparing, and are they independent or matched (paired)? And are the assumptions of the usual test reasonable, or do the data call for a test that makes fewer?

Your dataThe usual testIf its assumptions fail
a continuous outcome, two independent groupsa two-sample t-test (Welch’s): t.test(y ~ group)Wilcoxon rank-sum test: wilcox.test(y ~ group)
a continuous outcome, measured twice on the same peoplea paired t-test: t.test(after, before, paired = TRUE)Wilcoxon signed-rank test: wilcox.test(after, before, paired = TRUE)
a continuous outcome, three or more groupsone-way ANOVA: aov(y ~ group), then TukeyHSD()Kruskal–Wallis test: kruskal.test(y ~ group)
two categorical variables (a table of counts)chi-square test: chisq.test(table)Fisher’s exact test, when expected counts are small: fisher.test(table)
genotype counts against Hardy–Weinberg proportionschi-square test with 1 degree of freedom (hwe_p())an exact test (for example in the HardyWeinberg package), for rare alleles
two continuous variablesPearson’s correlation: cor.test(x, y) (next week: regression)Spearman’s correlation: cor.test(x, y, method = "spearman")

Two pieces of advice that matter more than the choice of test. First, decide on your main test before you look at the results: trying several tests and reporting the one with the smallest p-value makes p-values meaningless (more about this in week 6). Second, report the size of the effect, with its confidence interval, as well as the p-value.

Summary and homework

What you have learnt this week, the homework, and how much of the practical you have done.

This week’s main ideas

  • A test starts from a null hypothesis. The p-value is the probability, if H0 were true, of data at least as extreme as yours. It isn’t the probability that H0 is true, and it doesn’t measure the size of an effect.
  • A permutation test builds the null distribution by shuffling labels. The t-test uses the t distribution instead. Both ask how many standard errors apart two means are.
  • Check the assumptions (independence, roughly normal groups); use Wilcoxon or Kruskal–Wallis when they fail, and a paired test for matched measurements.
  • For three or more groups, use ANOVA, then Tukey’s test to see which groups differ.
  • For counts, use a chi-square test, or Fisher’s exact test when expected counts are small, and measure the association with an odds ratio.
  • Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium is a quality check of genotypes. Test it within groups of similar ancestry: mixing populations gives too few heterozygotes.
  • Always report the estimate and its confidence interval, not just the p-value.
